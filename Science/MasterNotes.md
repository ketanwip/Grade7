# Unit 5 — Ecology

## Part 1: Introduction to Ecology

### What Is Ecology?

**Teacher material:**
- **Ecology** is the study of how organisms interact with one another and with their environment.
- An **organism** is a living thing.

Ecology therefore examines both interactions among living things and interactions between living things and the nonliving environment.

> **Think Deeper:** An organism does not exist in isolation. Its survival can depend on other organisms (food, predators, competitors, decomposers) and on nonliving conditions such as water, temperature, sunlight, and soil.

## Characteristics of Living Things

### Core Characteristics

**Teacher material:** Living things:
1. **Are made of cells**
2. **Use energy**
3. **Grow and mature**
4. **Reproduce**
5. **Respond to their environment** (including maintaining **homeostasis**)
6. **Contain DNA**

### 1. Made of Cells

A **cell** is the basic unit of life/living things/organisms.

- **Unicellular organism:** made of **one cell**.
- **Multicellular organism:** made of **many cells**.

> **Think Deeper:** A single-celled organism still has to perform the functions needed for life. In multicellular organisms, different cells can become specialized for different jobs.

### 2. Use Energy

Living things **use energy** to carry out life processes.

The teacher notes introduce:
- **Autotroph:** an organism that **makes its own food**.
- **Heterotroph:** an organism that gets energy by **eating other organisms** or by **breaking down remains of dead organisms and absorbing nutrients**.

> **Important distinction:** "Makes its own food" does not mean an autotroph creates energy from nothing. It uses energy from its surroundings to make food.

### 3. Grow and Mature

Living things **grow and mature** as they develop.

> **Think Deeper:** Growth and maturation are related but not identical. Growth involves physical increase/change, while maturation involves developmental changes as an organism progresses through its life cycle.

### 4. Reproduce

Living things **reproduce**, producing new organisms.

**Asexual reproduction** (teacher material): reproduction involving **one parent** and producing **identical offspring**.

> **Think Deeper:** Asexual reproduction can allow rapid population growth, but producing very similar offspring means there may be less genetic variation when environmental conditions change.

### 5. Respond to the Environment / Maintain Homeostasis

Living things **respond to their environment** and maintain **homeostasis**.

- **Homeostasis:** maintaining relatively stable internal conditions even when the outside environment changes.

> **Think Deeper:** Homeostasis does not mean internal conditions never change. It means an organism regulates them within an appropriate range.

### 6. Contain DNA

Living things **contain DNA**. DNA carries genetic information involved in the structure, function, development, and reproduction of organisms.

> **Beyond the Basics:** DNA helps explain why offspring inherit characteristics from parents. Differences or changes in DNA can contribute to variation among individuals.

## Biotic and Abiotic Factors

### Biotic Factors

**Teacher material:** **Biotic factors** are living factors. The reading further defines biotic as **living or having lived** and includes organisms and remains of organisms.

Examples from the materials:
- Grass, bugs, dogs, humans
- Bacteria, fungi, trees
- Frogs, leaves, dead trees, pieces of wood
- Pollen from a plant

### Abiotic Factors

**Teacher material:** **Abiotic factors** are nonliving factors. The reading defines abiotic as **non-living, or never having lived**, and describes abiotic factors as nonliving, noncellular, inorganic materials contributing to an environment.

Examples from the materials:
- Rocks, sunlight, temperature, weather
- Water, oxygen, soil, sand
- Gold, brick, cement, bicycle
- Pollution from an oil refinery

### The Key Distinction

| Biotic | Abiotic |
|---|---|
| Living or having lived | Nonliving / never having lived |
| Organisms and remains of organisms | Nonliving components of the environment |
| Grass, bacteria, fungi, trees | Water, rocks, sunlight, temperature |
| Dead tree, leaf, wood | Sand, oxygen, soil |

### Common Trap: Dead Does Not Automatically Mean Abiotic

A **dead tree is still a biotic factor** according to the reading because it once lived. A leaf or piece of wood can likewise be biotic even after it is no longer alive.

A useful question is:

> **Did this thing live or come from something that lived?**

If yes, it is classified as **biotic** in the teacher material. If it is nonliving and never lived, it is **abiotic**.

### How Biotic and Abiotic Factors Affect Ecosystems

**Teacher material:** Both types of factors affect the **type and number of organisms** that can inhabit an ecosystem.

#### Desert Example

- **Abiotic factor:** lack of water.
- Lack of water prevents most trees from living in the desert because they need steady rainfall.
- **Biotic factor:** rodents such as mice.
- Rodents provide food that enables populations of predatory coyotes to survive in the harsh desert environment.

This shows that environmental factors influence which organisms can survive and how populations are supported.

> **Think Deeper:** An abiotic factor can have biological consequences. For example, temperature itself is nonliving, but a temperature change can affect survival, reproduction, competition, and population size.

### Biotic + Abiotic + Habitat

The practice material connects:
- **Biotic** → living parts of the environment
- **Abiotic** → nonliving parts of the environment
- **Habitat** → the place where an organism lives

Together, biotic and abiotic factors are important parts of the environment in which organisms live.

### Coral Reef Example

The reading asks for one abiotic and one biotic factor in a coral reef ecosystem. Examples consistent with the definitions include:
- **Abiotic:** water, sunlight, temperature, rocks
- **Biotic:** coral, fish, algae, other marine organisms

> **Challenge:** A dead coral skeleton is different from living coral tissue. When classifying a specific object, determine whether the material is living/once-living or never-living.

## Levels of Environmental Organization

### Overview

**Teacher material:** The five levels are ordered **from the simplest to the most complicated**:

1. **Individual organism**
2. **Population**
3. **Community**
4. **Ecosystem**
5. **Biosphere**

**Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biosphere**

### Level 1: Individual Organism

An **individual organism** is **a living thing**.

Examples: one dog, one tree, one bacterium, one human.

The key idea is **one individual living thing**.

### Level 2: Population

A **population** is a **group of organisms of the same species that live in a specific geographical area**.

Key requirements:
- More than one organism
- **Same species**
- A defined geographical area

**Teacher example:** The coconut trees on an island can represent a population when they are the same species living in that defined area.

> **Common Mistake:** A group containing different species is not a population. Different species living and interacting together points to **community**.

### Level 3: Community

A **community** is **all of the populations of different species that live in the same habitat and interact with each other**.

Example: an island community could include populations of coconut trees, insects, birds, fungi, and other organisms living and interacting there.

> **Think Deeper:** A community focuses on the living populations. When the nonliving environment is included, the level becomes an **ecosystem**.

### Level 4: Ecosystem

An **ecosystem** is **a community of organisms and their nonliving environment**.

**Ecosystem = living community + nonliving environment**

Example: a pond ecosystem includes its organisms plus water, sunlight, temperature, rocks, and other nonliving conditions.

> **Common Mistake:** An ecosystem is not just a collection of organisms. The **nonliving environment is part of the ecosystem**.

### Level 5: Biosphere

The **biosphere** is **all parts of Earth where life exists**.

It is the broadest of these five levels and includes Earth's many ecosystems wherever life exists.

> **Think Deeper:** The biosphere is the largest scale in this hierarchy. Ecosystems are not isolated from the rest of Earth; organisms, matter, and energy are connected through Earth's systems.

## Habitat and Niche

### Habitat

**Teacher material:** A **habitat** is **the place where an organism usually lives**.

Think of habitat as an organism's **address** — where it lives.

### Niche

**Teacher material:** A **niche** is **the role of a species in its community, including use of its habitat and its relationships with other species**.

Think of niche as the organism's **role/job** rather than simply its location.

A niche includes how a species uses its habitat and how it relates to other species.

### Habitat vs. Niche

| Concept | Main question |
|---|---|
| **Habitat** | **Where does it live?** |
| **Niche** | **What role does it play, and how does it interact with other species/environment?** |

> **Common Mistake:** Habitat and niche are not synonyms. Two organisms can share a habitat but have different niches because they use resources differently or have different relationships with other organisms.

> **Challenge:** If two species rely on exactly the same limited resources in exactly the same way, their niches overlap strongly, which can increase competition.

## Connecting the Major Ideas

These concepts form one connected ecological picture:

- **Characteristics of living things** → what makes an organism living.
- **Biotic and abiotic factors** → living/once-living and nonliving parts of the environment.
- **Habitat** → where an organism usually lives.
- **Niche** → its role and relationships within its community.
- **Levels of organization** → how organisms fit into increasingly larger systems.

### One Frog Example: Smallest to Largest

1. **Organism:** one frog
2. **Population:** frogs of the same species living in the pond/area
3. **Community:** frogs plus populations of other species interacting there
4. **Ecosystem:** that community plus water, sunlight, temperature, rocks, and other nonliving factors
5. **Biosphere:** all parts of Earth where life exists

The frog also has a **habitat** (where it lives) and a **niche** (its role and relationships).

## Common Classification Traps

### Population vs. Community
- **Same species** → Population
- **Different species/populations interacting** → Community

### Community vs. Ecosystem
- **Living populations** → Community
- **Living community + nonliving environment** → Ecosystem

### Ecosystem vs. Biosphere
- **Particular ecological system** → Ecosystem
- **All parts of Earth where life exists** → Biosphere

### Biotic vs. Abiotic
- **Living or having lived** → Biotic
- **Nonliving / never having lived** → Abiotic

### Habitat vs. Niche
- **Where it lives** → Habitat
- **Its role and relationships** → Niche

## Accelerated-Level Thinking

### Studying Ecology at Different Scales

The levels of organization provide different scales for studying life:

- **Organism:** How does one frog respond to temperature?
- **Population:** How many frogs of the same species live in the pond?
- **Community:** How do frogs interact with insects, plants, fish, and other organisms?
- **Ecosystem:** How do water temperature, sunlight, food availability, and organisms interact?
- **Biosphere:** How might large-scale environmental change affect where life can exist?

The same real-world event can therefore be studied at multiple levels.

### Feedback Within Ecosystems

Organisms are affected by their physical environment, but organisms can also change their environment. For example, plants can provide food and shelter, while organisms can alter soil or consume resources.

> **Beyond the Basics:** Ecosystems are systems of interacting components. Changing one important factor can produce effects elsewhere. For example, changing water availability can alter plant populations, which can then affect herbivores and predators.

### Homeostasis and Ecology

An organism responds to environmental changes while attempting to maintain stable internal conditions:

**Environment changes → organism detects/responds → organism attempts to maintain internal stability**

This connects a characteristic of individual living things to ecological conditions around them.
