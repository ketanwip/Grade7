# Grade 7 Science Notes: Ecology Part 2

## Unit 1 Ecology Part 2 — Energy Flow in Ecosystems

These notes follow the Canvas course label **Unit 1 Ecology Part 2**. They continue from the earlier ecology material by connecting population limits to the way energy moves through an ecosystem.

### Learning Goals

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

- classify organisms by how they obtain energy;
- trace energy through food chains and food webs;
- interpret the direction of arrows in feeding diagrams;
- identify trophic levels in an energy pyramid;
- use the 10% rule to compare energy at neighboring trophic levels; and
- explain how a change in one population can affect an entire ecosystem.

## 1. Population Limits: A Bridge from Part 1

### Carrying Capacity

**Carrying capacity** is the largest population of a species that an ecosystem can support over a long period with its available resources.

A population may grow quickly when resources are plentiful. As the population gets larger, competition and other limits increase. Growth then slows, and the population tends to level off or fluctuate near the carrying capacity.

### Limiting Factors

A **limiting factor** is anything that restricts population size or slows population growth.

| Biotic limiting factors | Abiotic limiting factors |
|---|---|
| competition | sunlight |
| predation | temperature and weather |
| disease and parasitism | water and nutrients |
| availability of mates | space, shelter, and soil chemistry |

A population is more likely to increase when it has more food, water, sunlight, and space, along with less competition and predation.

### Population Movement and Density

- **Immigration:** individuals move **into** a population.
- **Emigration:** individuals move **out of** a population.
- **Population density:** the number or concentration of individuals of one species in a particular area.

Immigration can increase a population, while emigration can decrease it. High population density may increase competition for limited resources.

## 2. Where Ecosystem Energy Begins

The **Sun is the ultimate source of energy** for most ecosystems. Producers capture light energy and transform it into chemical energy stored in food.

The **Law of Conservation of Energy** states that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another.

Energy does not cycle through an ecosystem in the same way that matter does. It enters mostly as sunlight, passes from organism to organism, and much of it is eventually released to the environment as heat.

## 3. Roles of Organisms

Organisms can be grouped by how they obtain energy.

### Producers and Autotrophs

A **producer** makes its own food using energy from its surroundings. Most producers use sunlight in **photosynthesis**. Plants, algae, and some bacteria are producers.

An **autotroph** is an organism that makes its own food. In this unit, producer and autotroph describe the same energy role.

### Consumers and Heterotrophs

A **consumer** gets energy by eating other organisms.

A **heterotroph** cannot make its own food and must obtain energy by consuming or absorbing other organisms or organic matter. Consumers and decomposers are heterotrophs.

Types of consumers include:

- **Herbivore:** eats plants or other producers.
- **Carnivore:** eats animals.
- **Omnivore:** eats both plants and animals.
- **Scavenger:** a specialized carnivore that feeds on the bodies of dead animals.

### Decomposers

A **decomposer** gets energy by breaking down or absorbing the remains and wastes of organisms. Decomposers return matter and nutrients to the environment. Fungi and many bacteria are decomposers.

**Scavenger versus decomposer:** a scavenger eats pieces of dead organisms, while a decomposer chemically breaks down remains and wastes into simpler substances. Both help recycle matter, but they do it differently.

## 4. Food Chains

A **food chain** is a diagram showing one path through which energy in food moves from one organism to another.

Example:

**Sun → grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk**

Important rules:

- Energy moves in **one direction** through a food chain.
- Producers form the base of food chains.
- An arrow points from the organism being eaten, or the energy source, **toward the organism receiving the energy**.
- Read an arrow as “provides energy to,” not simply “eats.”

For example, **grass → rabbit** means that energy stored in the grass is transferred to the rabbit when the rabbit eats it.

## 5. Food Webs

A **food web** is a diagram showing the connected feeding relationships among organisms in an ecosystem. It is made of many overlapping food chains.

Food webs are more realistic than single food chains because most organisms eat more than one kind of food and may be eaten by several predators.

To read a food web:

1. Start with a producer.
2. Follow each arrow in the direction that energy travels.
3. Identify all possible pathways from producers to consumers.
4. Remember that an organism may occupy different trophic levels in different chains, depending on what it eats.

### Interdependence and Ecosystem Balance

Because food-web relationships are connected, changing one population can affect many others.

The Yellowstone wolf example shows this idea. When wolves were reintroduced, they reduced parts of the elk population, especially old, injured, or diseased elk. With less browsing pressure, some plant populations increased. Those changes affected other organisms, including hares and foxes. The example demonstrates that predators can influence ecosystem balance far beyond their direct prey.

## 6. Trophic Levels and Energy Pyramids

A **trophic level** is an organism's feeding position in a food chain or food web.

An **energy pyramid** shows how much energy is available at each trophic level. Its broad base contains producers because that level has the greatest amount of energy. Each level above it is smaller because less energy is available.

| Trophic level | Typical role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | make food; form the base | grass |
| Primary consumers | eat producers; usually herbivores | rabbit |
| Secondary consumers | eat primary consumers; carnivores or omnivores | snake |
| Tertiary consumers | eat secondary consumers; often top predators | hawk |

Decomposers act on organisms and wastes from **every trophic level**, so they are not limited to only one layer of the pyramid.

### The 10% Rule

Only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level becomes available to the next trophic level. Much of the remaining energy is used for life processes and released as heat.

Teacher example:

| Level | Energy available |
|---|---:|
| Producers | 1,000 units |
| Primary consumers | 100 units |
| Secondary consumers | 10 units |
| Tertiary consumers | 1 unit |

This means tertiary consumers receive about **0.1%** of the energy that was available at the producer level.

### How to Calculate Energy Transfer

Moving **up** one trophic level:

`energy at next level = energy at current level × 0.10`

Moving **down** one trophic level:

`energy at lower level = energy at current level ÷ 0.10`

Example: If producers contain 50,000 kJ of energy, primary consumers receive about 5,000 kJ, secondary consumers about 500 kJ, and tertiary consumers about 50 kJ.

**Important:** The 10% rule describes transfer between trophic levels. Sunlight is the input to producers, but producers capture only a portion of the sunlight that reaches them. Do not automatically apply the 10% rule from the Sun to producers unless the teacher's problem explicitly tells you to use that convention.

## 7. Applying the Ideas: The Lion King Ecosystem

The Lion King activity combines several ecology concepts:

- **Producers:** grasses and trees capture solar energy.
- **Herbivores:** zebras, antelope, caterpillars, and other plant-eaters obtain energy from producers.
- **Carnivores and omnivores:** lions and other animals obtain energy from prey or a mixed diet.
- **Scavengers and decomposers:** organisms that consume or break down remains help return matter to the ecosystem.
- **Predator and prey:** a lion is a predator when it hunts an antelope; the antelope is its prey.
- **Food web:** many feeding paths connect the savanna organisms.
- **Succession:** after a disturbance, communities can change over time as organisms return and conditions improve.

“Flora dictates fauna” means that the plant life in an area strongly affects which animals can live there. Plants provide both the energy base and important habitat. When plant communities change, animal communities often change too.

## 8. Connections to Carrying Capacity

Energy availability helps determine carrying capacity.

- Producers determine how much energy enters the living part of an ecosystem.
- Because energy decreases at every trophic transfer, an ecosystem can support fewer organisms at higher trophic levels.
- A decrease in producers can reduce the carrying capacity for herbivores and then for predators.
- Competition increases when many organisms need the same limited food source.
- Changes in predators can alter prey populations and indirectly affect producers.

## 9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Reversing food-web arrows:** arrows point toward the organism receiving energy.
- **Calling every heterotroph a consumer:** decomposers are heterotrophs, but their role is to break down or absorb remains and wastes.
- **Confusing scavengers and decomposers:** scavengers eat dead animals; decomposers chemically break down organic material.
- **Thinking energy cycles:** matter cycles, but energy flows through the ecosystem and is gradually released as heat.
- **Using 90% instead of 10%:** about 10% transfers to the next trophic level; about 90% does not.
- **Putting the most organisms at the top of an energy pyramid:** the greatest energy supply is at the producer base.

## 10. Quick Vocabulary Review

- **Autotroph:** organism that makes its own food.
- **Heterotroph:** organism that obtains energy from other organisms or organic matter.
- **Producer:** autotroph that forms the energy base of most food chains.
- **Consumer:** organism that eats other organisms.
- **Decomposer:** organism that breaks down or absorbs remains and wastes.
- **Food chain:** one pathway of energy transfer.
- **Food web:** many connected food chains.
- **Trophic level:** feeding position in a chain or web.
- **Energy pyramid:** model of available energy at each trophic level.
- **Carrying capacity:** largest sustainable population in an ecosystem.
- **Limiting factor:** condition that restricts population size or growth.

## 11. Self-Check

1. Why are producers placed at the base of an energy pyramid?
2. In `algae → insect larva → fish → heron`, what is the role of the fish?
3. If primary consumers have 2,400 kJ, about how much energy is available to secondary consumers?
4. Why is a food web usually more realistic than a food chain?
5. How could a drought affect producers, herbivores, and carnivores?
6. What is the difference between a scavenger and a decomposer?

### Self-Check Answers

1. Producers introduce the greatest amount of usable energy into the living system by making food.
2. The fish is a secondary consumer because it eats a primary consumer.
3. About 240 kJ.
4. Most organisms have several food sources and predators, so many food chains overlap.
5. A drought reduces water and may reduce producer growth. Less producer energy can support fewer herbivores, which may then support fewer carnivores.
6. A scavenger eats dead animals; a decomposer chemically breaks down or absorbs remains and wastes.
