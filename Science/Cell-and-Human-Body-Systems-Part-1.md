# Grade 7 Science Notes: Cell and Human Body Systems Part 1

## Unit 2 Part 1 — Cell Structure and Function

Cells are the smallest units that can carry out the processes of life. Their structures work together as a system to obtain and use nutrients, grow, reproduce, make needed materials, maintain stable conditions, and process waste.

### Learning Goals

By the end of this part of the unit, you should be able to:

- explain the three statements of cell theory;
- compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells;
- identify structures shared by all cells;
- compare plant and animal cells;
- connect each major organelle's structure to its function; and
- explain how organelles interact to keep a cell alive.

## 1. What Is a Cell?

A **cell** is the smallest functional and structural unit of all living organisms.

- **Structural unit** means that living things are built from cells.
- **Functional unit** means that cells perform the processes needed for life.
- An **organism** is any living thing.

Living things are made of cells, obtain and use energy, grow and develop, reproduce, respond to their environment, contain DNA, and maintain **homeostasis**, or stable internal conditions.

### What Cells Need

The class materials summarize the basic needs of cells as:

- food or nutrients;
- water;
- energy; and
- waste removal.

Not every cell uses oxygen or sunlight, so those cannot replace the four broad needs above.

## 2. Why Most Cells Are Small

Cells exchange nutrients, gases, and wastes across their outer surface. As a cell grows, its **volume** increases faster than its **surface area**. A very large cell may not have enough membrane surface to move materials in and out quickly enough for its volume.

This relationship is called the **surface-area-to-volume ratio**.

- A higher ratio gives a cell more exchange surface for each unit of internal volume.
- Smaller cells usually have a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio than larger cells.
- Cells often remain small or divide before exchange becomes inefficient.

For a cube-shaped model cell with side length `s`:

- `surface area = 6s²`
- `volume = s³`
- `surface-area-to-volume ratio = 6s² : s³`, which simplifies to `6 : s`

As `s` increases, the ratio decreases.

## 3. Development of Cell Theory

| Approximate date | Scientist | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1590 | Hans and Zacharias Janssen | Produced an early compound microscope |
| 1665 | Robert Hooke | Described boxlike spaces in cork and called them cells |
| 1670s | Anton van Leeuwenhoek | Observed living microorganisms, including bacteria and protists |
| 1838 | Matthias Schleiden | Concluded that plants are made of cells |
| 1839 | Theodor Schwann | Concluded that animals are made of cells and helped establish the cell as life's basic unit |
| 1858 | Rudolf Virchow | Proposed that cells arise from existing cells |

### The Three Statements of Cell Theory

1. All organisms are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of all living things.
3. All cells come from existing cells.

The theory developed as microscopes improved and multiple scientists combined evidence over time. A **scientific theory** is a well-supported explanation, not a guess.

### Modern Extensions

The class materials also identify ideas included in modern cell theory:

- energy flow occurs within cells;
- hereditary information in DNA passes from cell to cell; and
- cells share a basic chemical composition.

## 4. Structures Shared by All Cells

Every cell has four fundamental components:

| Structure | Core function |
|---|---|
| Cell membrane | Forms a protective boundary and controls what enters and leaves |
| Cytoplasm | Contains the cell's internal fluid and structures; many reactions occur here |
| Ribosomes | Build proteins from amino acids using genetic instructions |
| DNA | Stores hereditary information and instructions for cell processes |

An **organelle** is a specialized structure within a cell that performs a particular function. Some organelles are surrounded by membranes; ribosomes are not.

## 5. Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

The main difference is how the cells store their DNA and whether they contain membrane-bound organelles.

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Absent | Present; enclosed by a membrane |
| DNA location | In the cytoplasm or nucleoid region | Mainly inside the nucleus |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Present |
| Typical size and organization | Smaller and simpler | Larger and more complex |
| Organisms | Bacteria and Archaea | Protists, fungi, plants, and animals |
| Number of cells in the organism | Unicellular | May be unicellular or multicellular |

Both cell types have DNA, cytoplasm, ribosomes, and a cell membrane.

**Memory aid:** “Pro” rhymes with “no” nucleus, while “Eu” rhymes with “do” have a nucleus. This is only a memory aid; both types still have genetic material.

## 6. Cell Structure and Function

A cell's **structure** is the arrangement of its parts. Its **function** is the job those parts carry out. Structure and function are connected: the shape, membranes, folds, and position of a structure help it perform its job.

Cells in multicellular organisms can become **specialized**. They contain the same basic genetic information but may differ in shape, organelle numbers, and activity depending on their role.

### Cell Membrane

The **cell membrane** is a flexible boundary around every cell.

- protects the cell from its environment;
- separates the cell's interior from the outside; and
- regulates the movement of materials into and out of the cell.

Its double layer of phospholipids is **selectively permeable**, which means it allows some materials to cross more easily than others.

### Cytoplasm and Cytoskeleton

The **cytoplasm** is the region inside the cell membrane that includes fluid and cell structures outside the nucleus. Many chemical reactions occur there.

The **cytoskeleton** is a network of protein filaments that:

- gives the cell shape and support;
- helps organize and move cell parts; and
- participates in movement and cell division.

### Nucleus and DNA

The **nucleus** is a membrane-bound organelle in eukaryotic cells that contains most of the cell's DNA. Nuclear pores allow instructions to leave the nucleus.

DNA contains directions for cell activities, including instructions for building proteins. The nucleus is often called the cell's **control center** because these genetic instructions help regulate growth, metabolism, and reproduction.

### Ribosomes

**Ribosomes** assemble amino acids into proteins. They may float freely in the cytoplasm or attach to rough endoplasmic reticulum. Ribosomes are not membrane-bound and occur in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.

## 7. Energy-Processing Organelles

### Mitochondria

**Mitochondria** are the main sites of **cellular respiration** in eukaryotic cells. During cellular respiration, cells use oxygen to release energy stored in food molecules such as glucose and transfer it to **ATP**. ATP supplies usable energy for cell processes.

Mitochondria have two membranes. Folds in the inner membrane increase surface area for reactions involved in cellular respiration.

### Chloroplasts

**Chloroplasts** occur in plant cells and some algae. They contain the green pigment **chlorophyll** and perform **photosynthesis**.

During photosynthesis, cells use light energy, carbon dioxide, and water to make sugar and oxygen. The sugar stores chemical energy that cells can later release through cellular respiration.

**Connection:** Chloroplasts capture energy in sugar; mitochondria release usable energy from food into ATP.

## 8. Manufacturing and Transport System

Several organelles cooperate to make, process, package, and deliver proteins.

1. DNA in the nucleus stores the protein instructions.
2. Instructions leave through nuclear pores.
3. Ribosomes assemble the protein.
4. **Rough endoplasmic reticulum** helps process and transport proteins made by attached ribosomes.
5. Transport vesicles carry materials to the **Golgi complex**.
6. The Golgi modifies, sorts, and packages the materials.
7. Vesicles deliver the finished materials elsewhere in the cell or to the cell membrane for release.

### Endoplasmic Reticulum

The **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** is a system of folded membranes in eukaryotic cells.

- **Rough ER** has ribosomes and helps process and transport proteins.
- **Smooth ER** lacks ribosomes, makes lipids, and helps break down harmful substances.

### Golgi Complex and Vesicles

The **Golgi complex** is a stack of flattened membranes that modifies, packages, and distributes proteins and lipids.

A **vesicle** is a small membrane-bound sac that transports or stores materials. Vesicles can move Golgi products within the cell or fuse with the cell membrane to release them.

## 9. Support, Storage, and Cleanup

### Cell Wall

A **cell wall** is a rigid layer outside the cell membrane that provides support, protection, and shape.

The core class comparison emphasizes cell walls in plants and many prokaryotes. The textbook and vocabulary activity also identify cell walls in algae, fungi, and most bacteria.

> **Source clarification:** “Plant and prokaryotic cells only” is a simplified classroom comparison. Fungi and many algae also have cell walls, and not every prokaryote has one.

### Vacuoles

A **vacuole** is a membrane-bound storage sac that may hold water, nutrients, enzymes, salts, or wastes.

- Plant cells usually have one **large central vacuole**.
- Water in the central vacuole helps support the plant cell.
- When a plant's central vacuoles lose water, the plant may wilt.
- Animal cells may contain smaller vacuoles or vesicles.

> **Source clarification:** One class slide says all cells have vacuoles. The textbook gives the more precise statement that vacuoles occur in most plant, animal, and fungal cells; they are not a universal structure of every cell.

### Lysosomes

**Lysosomes** are membrane-bound sacs containing digestive enzymes. They break down worn-out organelles, waste, food particles, and some foreign material. Useful substances can then be recycled by the cell.

For this course, lysosomes are emphasized as an animal-cell organelle.

> **Beyond the Basics:** Plant cells usually perform a similar digestive and recycling role with lytic vacuoles rather than the classic animal-cell lysosome.

## 10. Plant and Animal Cells

Both plant and animal cells are eukaryotic. They share a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, ribosomes, mitochondria, ER, Golgi complex, cytoskeleton, and vesicles.

| Mainly emphasized in plant cells | Mainly emphasized in animal cells |
|---|---|
| Cell wall | Lysosomes |
| Chloroplasts | No cell wall or chloroplasts |
| One large central vacuole | Smaller vacuoles or vesicles |
| More regular or boxlike shape | Often rounder or irregular shape |

The cell wall lies outside the cell membrane. A plant cell has both structures.

## 11. The Cell as a System

No organelle works alone. Cell survival depends on coordinated interactions.

- The cell membrane obtains needed materials and removes waste.
- DNA supplies instructions, and ribosomes use them to make proteins.
- ER, Golgi complex, and vesicles process and transport cell products.
- Mitochondria provide ATP that powers these activities.
- In plant cells, chloroplasts make sugar and the central vacuole supports the cell.
- Lysosomes or lytic vacuoles break down materials for disposal or reuse.

If one important structure fails, several processes can be disrupted. For example, damaged mitochondria reduce ATP production, leaving less usable energy for transport, growth, repair, and reproduction.

## 12. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Saying prokaryotes have no DNA:** they have DNA, but it is not enclosed in a nucleus.
- **Saying only plants have cell walls:** fungi, many algae, and most bacteria also have them.
- **Confusing cell wall and cell membrane:** the membrane regulates transport; the wall adds rigid support and protection.
- **Saying every organelle has a membrane:** ribosomes and the cytoskeleton are not membrane-bound.
- **Calling the nucleus the site of protein construction:** DNA instructions are stored there, but ribosomes build proteins.
- **Confusing photosynthesis and cellular respiration:** chloroplasts store light energy in sugar; mitochondria release energy from food into ATP.
- **Assuming bigger cells work better:** increasing size lowers the surface-area-to-volume ratio and can slow material exchange.

## 13. Quick Vocabulary Review

- **Cell:** smallest functional and structural unit of life.
- **Organelle:** specialized cell structure with a specific function.
- **Cell theory:** explanation stating that organisms consist of cells, cells are life's basic unit, and cells come from cells.
- **Prokaryote:** unicellular organism whose cell lacks a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
- **Eukaryote:** organism whose cells contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
- **Cell membrane:** selective boundary controlling movement into and out of a cell.
- **Cytoplasm:** internal region containing fluid and structures outside the nucleus.
- **DNA:** hereditary material containing instructions for cell processes.
- **Ribosome:** structure that builds proteins.
- **Nucleus:** eukaryotic organelle containing most cellular DNA.
- **Mitochondrion:** organelle where most cellular respiration occurs.
- **Chloroplast:** organelle where photosynthesis occurs.
- **Endoplasmic reticulum:** folded membrane system that makes or processes materials and helps transport them.
- **Golgi complex:** organelle that modifies, packages, and distributes cell products.
- **Vesicle:** small membrane-bound transport or storage sac.
- **Cytoskeleton:** protein-filament network providing shape, organization, and movement.
- **Vacuole:** membrane-bound storage sac.
- **Lysosome:** digestive organelle that breaks down materials.
- **Cell wall:** rigid outer layer providing support and protection.

## 14. Self-Check

1. Why does a smaller cell usually exchange materials more efficiently than a much larger cell?
2. What evidence led Hooke and Leeuwenhoek to different observations about cells?
3. State the three parts of cell theory.
4. Which four structures occur in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?
5. Why can a prokaryotic cell have DNA without having a nucleus?
6. Trace a protein from its genetic instructions to its delivery outside the cell.
7. How do chloroplasts and mitochondria connect energy capture and energy release?
8. Why does wilting often indicate water loss from plant-cell central vacuoles?
9. Predict two consequences if a cell's membrane stopped regulating transport.

### Self-Check Answers

1. A smaller cell has more surface area compared with its volume, so nutrients and wastes can cross the membrane efficiently.
2. Hooke examined dead cork and saw empty boxlike spaces; Leeuwenhoek used improved microscopes to observe living microorganisms.
3. All organisms consist of one or more cells; the cell is life's basic unit; all cells come from existing cells.
4. Cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, and DNA.
5. Its DNA is located in the cytoplasm or nucleoid region rather than inside a membrane-bound nucleus.
6. DNA instructions leave the nucleus, a ribosome builds the protein, rough ER processes and transports it, the Golgi modifies and packages it, and a vesicle carries it to the membrane.
7. Chloroplasts store light energy in sugar through photosynthesis; mitochondria release energy from food and transfer it to ATP through cellular respiration.
8. Water in the central vacuole presses outward and helps support the cell. Losing that water reduces support.
9. Needed materials might not enter at the correct rate, and waste or excess substances could accumulate; homeostasis would be disrupted.
